# Life Transitions Therapy on Long Island

A life transition is any change big enough to rearrange your days: a divorce, a new job or a lost one, leaving for college, retiring, moving, or bringing a baby home. Even a change you chose can leave you unsteady. At Pathways Within, licensed therapists help adults and families on Long Island get through these seasons in person and by telehealth.

If you are in crisis or thinking about harming yourself, call or text 988 (Veterans: press 1) or call 911.

## Signs it may be time to talk to someone

Change is normal. Needing help with it is also normal. People often reach out when:

- The change happened months ago and you still feel like you are waiting to land.
- You made the "right" decision and feel worse than you expected.
- Your sleep, appetite, or patience changed along with your circumstances.
- You keep replaying what you could have done differently.
- The people around you have moved on and you have not.
- You are facing a decision and cannot hear your own opinion over everyone else's.

Some transitions carry loss even when they look like gains. Retirement can mean losing a role you held for forty years. A move to Long Island for a partner's job can mean leaving every friend you had. Naming that loss is often the first thing a therapist helps with. When a transition follows a death, our [grief and loss](/concerns/grief-and-loss) page may fit better.

## How we treat life transitions at Pathways Within

Transitions are not an illness, so the goal is not to fix you. The goal is to help you make the decision in front of you, grieve what is ending, and build a routine that fits the new shape of your life. Your care plan draws on the services below.

[Individual therapy](/therapy/individual-therapy) gives you one hour a week that is only about you. Your therapist helps you separate the practical problems from the emotional ones and work on each. Several of our clinicians list life transitions as a specialty.

[Coaching](/coaching) fits when you know what you want and need help getting there, such as a career change or a return to work after having children. Coaching is forward looking and goal based. The Welcome Team helps you decide whether coaching, therapy, or both fits.

[Family therapy](/therapy/family-therapy) helps when the change lands on everyone: a divorce with children, a parent moving in, or a young adult leaving home or moving back. Our marriage and family therapists work with the whole household on how roles and rules shift.

[Couples therapy](/therapy/couples-therapy) helps partners who are changing at different speeds. New parenthood, a job relocation, or one partner's retirement can strain even a strong relationship. A couples therapist helps you talk about it before resentment sets in. Partners who are ending a relationship can also use couples therapy to separate with less damage.

Young adults leaving for college, or coming home from it, may prefer to start with [teen and young adult therapy](/therapy/teen-therapy). New and expecting parents can read more on our [postpartum and perinatal](/concerns/postpartum-and-perinatal) page.

## What your first weeks look like

You contact the Welcome Team by form, call, or text. A team member sets up your 360 intake, a conversation about what is changing and what you want to be different. If more than one person in your family is affected, say so. The team can plan for individual and family work at once.

Your first session with your therapist covers the story of the change, what you have already tried, and what a good outcome would look like. By the third session, most clients have a clearer picture of which parts of the transition are decisions, which are losses, and which are just time. The Welcome Team remains your contact for scheduling, and you can read the full process at [how it works](/how-it-works).

## Medication, therapy, or both

Most people going through a transition do not need medication. Therapy alone is usually the right plan. When a change brings on depression, panic, or weeks of poor sleep, medication can help you do the work of therapy. Our psychiatric nurse practitioner provides [medication management](/medication-management) for adolescents ages 10 and up and adults. Your prescriber and your therapist coordinate with your permission, and you can stop the conversation at any point.

## Common questions

### Is it too small a problem for therapy?

No. Therapists work with people who are not in crisis every day. If a change is taking up more of your mind than you want it to, that is reason enough. Short term therapy, sometimes eight to twelve sessions, is common for a single transition.

### Should I start therapy before or after the change?

Either works. Starting before a planned change, such as retirement or a move, lets you prepare. Starting after gives you somewhere to bring what actually happened. Many clients start in the middle, when the change is under way and nothing feels settled.

### Can my spouse or my kids come too?

Yes. The Welcome Team can plan individual, couples, and family sessions within one care plan. Some families start with family therapy and add individual sessions for one person. Others do the reverse. Your therapist helps you decide who should be in the room for what.

### What is the difference between coaching and therapy for a career change?

Coaching focuses on goals and next steps, such as a resume, an interview, or a decision between two offers. Therapy also works on what the change stirs up, such as fear, identity, or old patterns. If the career change is tangled up with anxiety or depression, therapy comes first.

### Does insurance cover therapy for life transitions?

Therapy is billed as psychotherapy, and most plans that cover mental health include it. Coverage varies by provider, service, and plan. The Welcome Team verifies benefits after your 360 intake. Coaching is self pay. See [insurance and fees](/insurance-and-fees).
